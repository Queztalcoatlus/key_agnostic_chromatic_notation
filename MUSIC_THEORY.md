# Music Theory in CN12

This document starts where the [README](README.md) leaves off: choosing
chords, connecting them, and building progressions.

Examples use labels such as **10 (A) major**. The number is the CN12 root;
the letter in parentheses is its conventional note name. The notes of each
chord are listed in CN12.

## Choosing Chords from a Scale

Start with the major scale on `10` (A). Take a note, skip the next scale note,
take another, skip one, and take a third. Continue into the next octave
when necessary.

Starting on `12` (B), this selects `12 3 7`. The gaps are `+0 +3 +4`,
so the result is a minor chord.

Doing this from each note gives:

| Root | Chord | Notes |
| ---- | ----- | ----- |
| `10` | 10 (A) major | `10 2 5` |
| `12` | 12 (B) minor | `12 3 7` |
| `2` | 2 (C#) minor | `2 5 9` |
| `3` | 3 (D) major | `3 7 10` |
| `5` | 5 (E) major | `5 9 12` |
| `7` | 7 (F#) minor | `7 10 2` |
| `9` | 9 (G#) diminished | `9 12 3` |

These chords all use notes from the scale. That gives us a starting set
of chords to combine; we can also use notes outside it.

## Making a Chord Feel Like Home

Play this sequence, holding each chord for four beats:

| Step | Chord | Notes |
| ---- | ----- | ----- |
| 1 | 10 (A) major | `10 2 5` |
| 2 | 3 (D) major | `3 7 10` |
| 3 | 5 (E) dominant seventh | `5 9 12 3` |
| 4 | 10 (A) major | `10 2 5` |

Repeat it, then stop on the final chord. Does that ending feel settled?
Now stop on the third chord. Does it feel as though another chord should
follow?

When `10` (A) feels like the resting point, we call it the **tonal center**.
Returning to its major chord helps establish it. The melody and timing
matter too: a center is something the music establishes, not something
the first note automatically decides.

To use this progression with another center, place its chord roots
`0, 5, 7, 0` semitones above that center and keep the same chord types.
For example, with center `1` (C), the chords are **1 (C) major, 6 (F) major,
8 (G) dominant seventh, 1 (C) major**.

## Connecting Individual Notes

A chord change is also several individual notes moving at once. Following
those movements is called **voice leading**.

Compare **3 (D) major** with **10 (A) major** from the progression above:

| Note in 3 (D) Major | Note in 10 (A) Major | Movement |
| --------------- | --------------- | -------- |
| `3` | `2` | Down 1 semitone |
| `7` | `5` | Down 2 semitones |
| `10` | `10` | Hold the same note |

Play the destination notes in the nearby octaves indicated by these
movements. You can connect the chords with two small moves and one held
note, even though their roots are five semitones apart.

Choosing which octave each note occupies is part of choosing a **voicing**.
You do not have to put the root at the bottom of every chord.

### Why the Seventh Chord Can Lead Home

Now look at **5 (E) dominant seventh -> 10 (A) major**:

`5 9 12 3 -> 10 2 5`

Two notes can approach the destination by a single semitone:

- `9 -> 10`, upward.
- `3 -> 2`, downward.

Play those two movements together, then add the other chord notes. This
is one way to hear the connection between the chords. The upward step
`9 -> 10` is called a **leading-tone** movement.

In this passage, the seventh chord has **dominant function**: it helps
lead back to the center. That describes its job here. The same chord
could have a different job in another passage.

Small movements are useful connections, not a formula that makes every
progression sound finished. Try delaying the final chord or ending the
melody on a different note and listen to what changes.

## A Three-Chord Progression

Try this sequence:

| Chord | Notes |
| ----- | ----- |
| 12 (B) minor seventh | `12 3 7 10` |
| 5 (E) dominant seventh | `5 9 12 3` |
| 10 (A) major seventh | `10 2 5 9` |

The first change can hold `12` and `3`. The second can hold `5` and
`9`. Keeping these notes in place lets the remaining notes move around
them.

Notice that `9` can stay in the final major-seventh chord. It does not
have to rise to `10` as it did in the previous example.

The roots are `2, 7, 0` semitones above the center. To play the same
progression around center `1` (C), use **3 (D) minor seventh -> 8 (G) dominant
seventh -> 1 (C) major seventh**.

## Changing One Note in Minor

Make **10 (A) minor**, `10 1 5`, the home chord. Compare these two endings:

| Ending | Notes |
| ------ | ----- |
| 5 (E) minor -> 10 (A) minor | `5 8 12 -> 10 1 5` |
| 5 (E) major -> 10 (A) minor | `5 9 12 -> 10 1 5` |

Only one note in the first chord changes: `8` becomes `9`.
Now it is one semitone below the destination `10`, instead of two.
Listen to how that changes the approach to the home chord.

Adding `3` to the 5 (E) major chord makes **5 (E) dominant seventh**.
It can also lead to **10 (A) minor**.

The note `9` is outside the natural minor scale on `10` (A). We use it here
because of how it moves. A passage does not have to stay inside a single
scale.

Another useful change is **3 (D) major -> 3 (D) minor -> 10 (A) major**:

`3 7 10 -> 3 6 10 -> 10 2 5`

First, `7` falls to `6`. Then `6` can fall to `5`, while
`3` falls to `2` and `10` stays in place. A note outside the major
scale gives us a descending line into the final chord.

## Writing a Loop

Play these chords repeatedly, one bar each:

**10 (A) major -> 5 (E) major -> 7 (F#) minor -> 3 (D) major -> repeat**

| Chord | Notes |
| ----- | ----- |
| 10 (A) major | `10 2 5` |
| 5 (E) major | `5 9 12` |
| 7 (F#) minor | `7 10 2` |
| 3 (D) major | `3 7 10` |

The roots are `0, 7, 9, 5` semitones above center `10` (A).

A loop can keep moving without a strong ending on every repetition.
Try holding 10 (A) major for two bars, or holding 7 (F#) minor for two bars.
Changing how long a chord lasts changes its emphasis. The timing of
chord changes is called **harmonic rhythm**.

The melody can also emphasize `10` (A) or `7` (F#). Although these chords use
the same notes as both relative scales, the notes alone do not settle
which center you will hear.

## Leading to Another Chord

We can use the same kind of approach to emphasize a chord other than home:

**10 (A) major -> 12 (B) dominant seventh -> 5 (E) major -> 10 (A) major**

The middle change is:

`12 4 7 10 -> 5 9 12`

Here, `4` can rise to `5`, and `10` can fall to `9`, both by one
semitone. The seventh chord leads toward **5 (E) major**, just as **5 (E) dominant
seventh** led toward **10 (A) major** earlier.

The general pattern is a dominant-seventh chord rooted seven semitones
above its target. For target `5` (E), that root is `5 + 7 = 12`.

Briefly emphasizing `5` (E) does not necessarily make it the new center.
If the following music keeps returning to `5` and treating it as home,
we have changed centers. That is **modulation**.

## Replacing a Chord While Keeping Its Main Movements

Compare these two chords:

| Chord | Notes |
| ----- | ----- |
| 5 (E) dominant seventh | `5 9 12 3` |
| 11 (Bb) dominant seventh | `11 3 6 9` |

Both contain `3` and `9`. Either chord lets those notes move
`3 -> 2` and `9 -> 10` into **10 (A) major**.

Try both endings:

**5 (E) dominant seventh -> 10 (A) major**

**11 (Bb) dominant seventh -> 10 (A) major**

In the second, the bass can also descend one semitone, `11 -> 10`.
The replacement changes the other notes, so listen to it with the melody
before deciding whether it works.

The roots `5` and `11` are six semitones apart, a tritone. This
replacement is called **tritone substitution**.
